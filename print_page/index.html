    IBM Bob Level 3

    i. What is IBM Bob?

    IBM Bob is an AI-powered partner for the entire software development lifecycle (SDLC), and it lives where your developers already work: inside the development environment itself. It is tempting to file Bob alongside the many code-completion assistants that have flooded the marketplace since LLMs arrived — but that comparison sells it short. Bob is not simply a code generator, but a partner for every step in your weekly workflow (for developers and sellers, alike.)

    A code generator helps at the exact point in time that a function gets written. Bob is built to help across the whole arc of a project: planning a feature before a line of code exists, reasoning about an unfamiliar codebase, implementing changes, and automating the repetitive work that surrounds real development.

    A WORD ON WORKING WITH GENERATIVE AI

    IBM Bob is powered by generative AI and LLMs, and a defining trait of these systems is that they are non-deterministic — unlike the deterministic tools most developers are accustomed to. In practice, that means the same prompt can produce different code from one run to the next. This is both a strength and a quirk of the technology, and it is something to work with rather than against.

    Precision is what tips the odds in your favor. The more clearly you describe what you want, the more closely Bob's output will mirror your intent; conversely, the smallest change in wording can meaningfully change the generated code. Throughout this lab, your results may differ from the solutions shown — and that is expected, not a defect. Whenever an output diverges, you have three good options: refine the prompt and try again, edit the generated code by hand, or simply proceed if the variation still compiles and runs. Human review remains essential at every step, and never more so than while you are still learning the tool.


    At its core, Bob does three things exceptionally well:

    • Writes and modifies code across an entire codebase, from natural language instructions
    • Understands codebases by reading and reasoning about complex project structures, dependencies, and architectural patterns
    • Automates the busy-work of development: from documentation, to commit messages, to test scaffolding

    ii. Bob across the software development lifecycle

    A distinction worth holding onto is scope. Where a traditional AI assistant is anchored to the moment of writing or fixing code, Bob is designed to support the lifecycle around it — helping you plan features before implementation begins, understand the full context of a project before making changes, and keep the developer in control at every step. Bob augments the way your teams already work; it does not replace their judgment. Keep ahold of that last point as you work your way through the remainder of the hands-on material, as it is the thread that weaves together so many of Bob's strengths and its value to IBM clients.

    iii. Where Bob meets you: the IDE and the Shell

    Bob comes in two form factors to meet developers wherever they are most productive:

    Interface Description
    Bob IDE A VS Code extension providing an agentic sidebar, inline code assistance, and mode-based interaction
    Bob Shell A terminal-based interface offering the same capabilities in a keyboard-driven, automation-friendly form

    You will spend most of this course in the Bob IDE, where the Visual Studio Code-like user interface of the Bob IDE will already feel at-home to many developers and users. In the final module of the hands-on lab, you'll have the opportunity to briefly touch upon the Bob Shell interface — where you will find that everything learned up to that point also applies programatically as well.

    iv. Curriculum: AI-assisted code maturity curve

    The four modules constituting the hands-on work ahead trace the AI-assisted code maturity curve — and as you shift right along it, you will progressively hand Bob more responsibility, with Bob earning that trust by keeping you (the human) in command the entire way.

    Module Stage What it means for your developers — and your clients
    1 Assist Bob accelerates everyday work in the editor, and you accept or reject every change. Velocity from day one with near-zero risk.
    2 Delegate Bob takes a whole feature from idea to working code, but builds only what you've reviewed and approved first.
    3 Tailor Bob learns your codebase, enforces your standards, and adapts to each role, so it behaves like a teammate who already knows how you work.
    4 Scale & Extend Bob stays reliable on large, long-running work and plugs into the tools your enterprise already runs.

    A core, unifying theme of the journey you're embarking upon: the developer always stays in control. At the Assist stage, control is regulated as you choose to accept (or reject) each generative AI-suggested code recommendation, keystroke by keystroke. Within the Delegate module, control is maintained through approval of Bob-generated plans before a single line of AI-generated code is written. At Tailor, it means encoding the very rules Bob must adhere to. Within the Scale & Extend module, it means governing context, credentials, and tool access.

    As you and Bob together progress right along the maturity curve, capabilities will expand as trust grows. That escalation is the story — and it is the story your sellers can carry into a C-suite conversation: not "here are some features," but "here is how your teams grow into an AI partner without losing agency to an AI agent."

    TAB COMPLETION & NEXT EDIT PREDICTION

    i. Bob in your workflow

    This is the first (and most gentle) stage of the AI maturity curve, and therefore a natural sweet spot for building your understanding of IBM Bob. Across this module, you'll work shoulder to shoulder with Bob from the perspective of a software developer paired with the Bob editor. As you work, Bob will be anticipating the next keystroke, drafting the next few lines, and making targeted edits upon your request — all the while keeping the developer (you) in control of accepting or rejecting every AI-generated proposed change.

    The value that IBM Bob provides in this will be apparenty right from the onset: teams can move faster without breaking their train of thought, and nothing lands in the codebase that they did not explicitly choose to include. It is the most logical on-ramp to working with an AI partner, and it pays off from the very first session.

    Watch first: Chapter 2.1: Next Edit Prediction and Tab Completion [IBM Bob L3]

    Maximilian Jesch (Outbound Product Manager — IBM Bob) demonstrates Bob's tab completion and next edit predictions. [1 min]

    ii. Why this matters

    The quickest way to lose a developer's confidence in an AI tool is to pull them out of their workflow.

    Bob's first two capabilities are designed to do precisely the opposite: it keeps a developer in the work, while quietly removing friction from the process. These two features are:

    1. Tab completion
    2. Next edit prediction

    The two capabilities operate as a unified pair: the first suggests code from context; the second anticipates where you will need to go next.

    How tab completion works

    Tab completion is a familiar idea executed unusually well. Where conventional autocomplete reacts to the few characters you have just typed, Bob reads the surrounding context to make a genuinely informed suggestion — drawing on the comments describing your intent, the patterns and structure already present in the code, your project's conventions and style, and related code in other files. The result feels less like word-completion and more like a colleague finishing your thought.

    Accepting suggestions

    When Bob offers a suggestion, the human in the loop retains control and has the last word on what is implemented:

    • Press Tab to accept it
    • Keep typing to override the AI-generated suggestions
    • Watch it update dynamically as you continue your train of thought

    Next edit predictions

    Real code changes are rarely confined to a single line within the codebase. Add a new field or variable to the code, and you'll oftentime find yourself needing to use it again elsewhere; introduce a function, and something downstream will need to call it. Next edit prediction is built around that practical reality: after you accept an AI-generated suggestion, Bob anticipates the related edit you are likely to need as a follow-up and moves you there.

    You can conceptualize this as a rhythm between the developer and their AI partner. The developer describes what they want in a comment, allows Bob to suggest the code fix, presses Tab to accept the suggestion, and presses Tab again to jump to the next predicted location — where Bob positions the cursor exactly where the new code needs to be invoked.

    LITERATE CODING

    i. Editing with intent

    Tab completion and next edit prediction shine while you are writing new code. But much of a developer's day is spent making precise, surgical change to code that already exists. To that end, literate coding is the tool of choice. It lets you annotate— using plain, natural language instructions — direct inside of a file where you needed the changes to occur. Bob then takes care of the heavy lifting by ensuring that every change is accounted for and that no instructions are missed.

    IBM Bob reads the instruction in place and produces a targeted, localized modification. The developer is still describing intent in plain language; the difference is that here you are pointing at a specific spot and saying "change this." Think of it like annotating changes in a collaborative Word document, where you can delegate the modifications to a trusted colleague who will shoulder the workload.

    Watch first: Chapter 2.2: Literate Coding [IBM Bob L3]

    Maximilian Jesch (Outbound Product Manager — IBM Bob) demonstrates Bob's literate coding capabilities. [2 min]

    ii. Why this matters

    Literate coding is at its best for focused, in-place work:

    • Wrapping logic around existing code — retry mechanisms, error handling, or logging
    • Refactoring a specific block while leaving the surrounding logic untouched
    • Quick, targeted modifications to a single function without disturbing the broader structure

    IBM Bob users engage the literate coding mode through the magic wand icon in the Bob IDE (you'll see exactly how this is performed in a few moments.) Once active, it allows those users to write natural-language instructions at a precise code location. They can target the exact section of code where changes are to be made; alternatively, they can queue up several instructions in sequence, across multiple locations of a single file.

    AI MATURITY CURVE

    Literate Coding is a prime example of where the control that the human in the loop retains with IBM Bob — as part of the early Assist stage along the AI maturity curve — becomes tangible and truly valuable. IBM Bob never silently rewrites your files or codebase. Instead, it presents every change in a diff view that shows the added code, the removed code, and enough surrounding context to understand the scope of what is changing. You review, then you decide on whether to implement the suggestion or disregard it. Nothing is committed until you say so.

    How it works

    The workflow is deliberately simple:

    1. Position your cursor within the codebase where the change belongs
    2. Click the magic wand icon to enter literate coding mode
    3. Type a natural-language instruction describing the change you want to implement
    4. For changes that span several places, add an instruction line at each location
    5. Click generate to produce the modifications (across one or many instructions)

    Going further

    For sweeping, multi-file changes or architectural restructuring, you will instead want to reach for IBM Bob's Code mode. The topic is covered extensively within MODULE 2 | DELEGATE, which follows this section.

    EXPECT SOME VARIABILITY

    Generative AI is non-deterministic and as such your results may differ from what you see here. If an output diverges, consider further refining the prompt, following up with another prompt, or adjusting the code by hand. Iteration and further precision are often the best remedies.

    THE AGENTIC SIDEBAR

    i. From assistant to partner

    In the previous Assist module, you engaged with IBM Bob in its most "manual" and "hands-on" form: it finished lines of code for you, predicted the next set of edits to be performed, and made surgical changes (with your approval) sequentially throughout a codebase. From the end-user (your) perspective, the experience thus far has felt closely watched and tightly chaperoned. Every action Bob has taken has been directly instrumented by you, or at the very least required your direct sign-off at every step in the sequence, to ensure no changes were made to the code base without your expressed permission.

    AI MATURITY CURVE

    This makes sense when viewed through the lens of the AI Maturity Curve that was discussed previously. Where you are currently in your journey with IBM Bob— progressing from the nascent Assist stage on the left-most portion of the curve, moving further to the right into the Delegate phase —is at a point where trust in the AI technology should be low. Coordinating code changes with IBM Bob with a healthy degree of skepticism is a valid way to safely vet the AI partner's capabilities, build trust in its ability to deliver the results you seek, and gradually begin delegating more responsibility to that agent.

    The Delegate module ahead raises those stakes considerably and asks for a much higher degree of trust on the user's (your) part. The work ahead will transform your perspective of IBM Bob from an assistant into a trusted partner. To earn that trust, Bob will demonstrate taking an entire feature of the Galaxium travel app— one that spans the frontend, the backend, and the database —and shephard it from idea to working code, largely on its own.

    The reason delegation of this magnitude remains safe— and why you should feel confident in demonstrating this to even a cautious or skeptical prospective enterprise audience —is the core discipline of IBM Bob that was introduced earlier, and will be reinforced here: Bob builds only what you have reviewed and approved in a plan first. Human control does not degrade as the stakes rise. Control remains anchored from the start of the process and at the onset of the AI Maturity Curve, with the plan of execution you establish for Bob, your AI partner.

    All work you have performed across the hands-on lab so far has been inline coding modifications: IBM Bob performing work at the exact spot your cursor sits.     • Read through files

    Where the Next Edit Prediction and Literate Coding assist functionality that was showcased in the previous module required your direct input— keystroke by keystroke —the Agentic Sidebar allows you hand over and delegate entire objectives (and very complex workflows) with a single request.     UNDERSTANDING THE VARIOUS MODES

    The agentic sidebar offers several modes, each tuned for a specific phase of development and code lifecycle management.

    For substantial feature work, the recommended workflow is a deliberate sequence:
    Plan mode → review → new conversation → Code mode

    Each step of this sequence (from plan, review, converse, and code) exists deliberately as part of a methodical workflow. Before going hands-on with the agentic sidebar, it's worth taking a moment first to explore the "sweet spot" use cases for each mode.

    iv. Plan Mode | think before building

    Plan mode is the starting point for any feature or architectural change of consequence. In this mode Bob has read-only access to the codebase — it cannot change anything — and its sole job is to produce a detailed plan before a single line of code is written. Reach for it whenever a change touches multiple files or layers, when you are designing new components or data models, or when you simply want to understand the scope of a change before committing to it.

    Entering it is straightforward: open the agentic sidebar in VS Code, select Plan from the mode selector, and describe the feature in natural language. From there, Bob reads the relevant files to understand the existing structure, asks clarifying questions to resolve ambiguity before planning, and then generates planning documents — typically two: an implementation plan (a detailed specification of what the feature does, how it will be built, how it will be tested, and how it should behave — often 100 to 200-plus lines) and an architecture plan (a higher-level account of structural decisions, component interactions, and data flow). These documents are saved to the project and become the authoritative specification for the build.

    v. Review and Plan | a developer's responsibility

    The review and planning process is at the core of everything you (the user) performs with the service. The plans that IBM Bob generates are not loose suggestions — they are exactly what Bob will build, verbatim. Approving a plan you have not adequately reviewed or read ahead of time will hand full responsibility for any misaligned or incorrect implementation squarely to you. Bob, as they say, was only following orders.

    Before you proceed with delegating tasks to Bob, it's imperative that you read both the implementation plan and the architecture plan in full. Verify that the described behavior matches the feature you intended. Confirm that the testing requirements make sense. Correct any misunderstanding now, by revising your prompt or asking a follow-up, while correction is cheap (and far less costly a fix than after post-implementation.)

    RESPONSIBILITY AS A DEVELOPER AND END-USER

    Read the plans. To re-emphasize (and this is true across all LLMs): human-in-the-loop review is the single most important quality gate in any agentic workflow. The quality of the final implementation is directly tied to the quality of the plan you approve.

    vi. Code Mode | implementing the approved plan

    In Code mode, Bob has full access to file operations and command execution. It can create, modify, and run code across an entire codebase.

    Once the plan is reviewed and approved, the implementation happens withinin a new conversation. That detail is not incidental. Starting fresh clears Bob's context window, which keeps the implementation focused, prevents the earlier planning discussion from eating into the token budget, and reduces the chance of Bob conflating strategizing plans with implementing instructions. To carry the specification into the new conversation, you will need to reference the planning documents with context mentions. For example, the following two paths specify contextual details about a (hypothethical) implementation and architecture plan.

    ``` bash
@/path/to/implementation-plan.md
@/path/to/architecture-plan.md
```

    The process for "switching" into Code mode is straightforward and simple— and you'll have the opportunity to practice this yourself in the following hands-on steps — for reference:

    • Start a new conversation
    • Select Code from the mode selector
    • Optionally reference any relevant planning documents or context with @/path/to/file
    • Send the implementation request to Bob, which will carry out the task.

    vii. Auto-Approval | control, dialed to taste

    By default, Bob will ask for permission before performing consequential actions like writing files, running Terminal commands, or executing tests. The auto-approval setting governs whether those actions proceed automatically or pause for your review, with various configurations you can set to determine the level of autonomy (and trust) you are comfortable delegating to Bob.

    Setting Behavior When to use
    Manual approval Every action requires explicit confirmation Sensitive codebases, unfamiliar tasks
    Auto-approve Bob proceeds without interruption Controlled demos, trusted environments
    Hybrid Auto-approve low-risk actions; prompt for riskier ones Day-to-day development

    The convenience of full auto-approval comes at the trade-off of escalating exposure risk. If autonomy is dialed up to the highest degree, Bob will be able to run arbitrary Terminal commands without human review — reducing the management overhead on human users, but potentially introducing mistakes into a plan that can propagate unchecked. For most work, a hybrid and balanced posture is the sensible default — auto-approve file reads and writes, but require confirmation before command execution.

    Set auto-approval in Bob's settings panel according to the sensitivity of the project and the trust you place in the approved plan.

    viii. Referencing files | context mentions

    When you point Bob at files in the sidebar, use context mentions rather than copying and pasting their contents:

    Mention syntax Effect
    @/path/to/file.js Includes the full contents of a specific file
    @/path/to/folder Includes all files within a directory
    @problems Includes current VS Code Problems panel diagnostics
    @terminal Includes recent terminal output

    Mentions are resolved at the moment you send the message, giving Bob precise, targeted information without burning context on unrelated files.
